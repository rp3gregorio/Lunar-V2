\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The deep thermal conductivity of lunar regolith, $K_d$, sets the
subsurface equilibrium temperature and hence the stability depth of
polar volatiles and the heat budget of buried instruments. Existing
constraints derive almost entirely from orbital brightness
temperatures and treat $K_d$ as globally uniform. We re-analyse the
restored 1971--1977 Apollo Heat-Flow Experiment (HFE) record at
Apollo~15 and~17, retrieving $K_d$ by integrating the 1-D heat
equation under ephemeris-driven solar forcing with shallow sensors
excluded \emph{a priori}. At the published basal heat flux $Q_b$ the
deep-sensor RMSE is minimised at
$K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}=4.9$ and
$K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=11.2$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$
($1\sigma$ non-parametric bootstrap): the two boreholes require
markedly different deep temperature gradients. Because that gradient
is governed by the ratio $Q_b/K_d$, a steeper gradient at A17 can
arise from either a higher conductivity or a higher basal flux, and
the fluxes are themselves contested. A pooled model comparison
resolves this: a variable-$K_d$ model is preferred over a uniform-$K_d$
model with site-specific $Q_b$, but only by
$\Delta\mathrm{AICc}\approx5$ --- enough to disfavour the uniform-$K_d$
alternative, not to reject it --- and that alternative moreover
requires the two basal fluxes at opposite extremes of their published
ranges. The HFE record therefore \emph{favours} an inter-site
conductivity contrast of a factor $\sim\!2$ but does not establish it
against uniform $K_d$ with differing flux. Either way, the in-situ
record is inconsistent with a globally-uniform deep regolith
\emph{thermal state}: analyses adopting both a global $K_d$ and a
global $Q_b$ should carry an uncertainty no smaller than this
$\sim\!2\times$ spread. The result is robust to the borestem
exclusion depth, a discrete 3-layer conductivity profile, and
independent Diviner surface-temperature closure.
\end{abstract}

\begin{plainlanguagesummary}
How effectively the Moon's interior is insulated from the daily
surface heating depends largely on the deep thermal conductivity of
compacted regolith (broken-up rock). Because this property has never
been measured directly from the surface, lunar spacecraft analyses
generally assume it is the same everywhere on the Moon. We re-examine
that assumption using 1971--1977 temperatures from thermometers that
the Apollo~15 and Apollo~17 astronauts buried more than two metres
below the surface, restored from the original tapes only recently.
The two landing sites clearly require different deep temperature
gradients. That difference can be explained either by the regolith
conducting heat differently at the two sites, or by different amounts
of heat flowing up from the Moon's interior --- and a single borehole
cannot tell those two causes apart. Comparing the possibilities, the
data lean toward a genuine difference in conductivity, of roughly a
factor of two, but do not firmly rule out the alternative. Either
way, the two in-situ records are not consistent with the Moon's deep
regolith being thermally uniform. Analyses that assume a single global
value --- including polar-ice-stability calculations and the heat
budgets of future landed instruments --- should therefore carry an
uncertainty at least as large as the roughly two-fold spread we
report here.
\end{plainlanguagesummary}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Key Points}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The Apollo 15 and 17 deep heat-flow records require markedly
        different deep temperature gradients.
  \item A model comparison favours an inter-site conductivity contrast
        of about a factor of two, but does not decisively exclude a
        uniform conductivity with differing basal heat flux.
  \item A single globally-uniform deep regolith thermal state is
        inconsistent with the two in-situ records.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Surface and shallow-subsurface temperatures of airless bodies are
controlled by the thermal conductivity, density, and heat capacity of
the upper $\sim\!2$\,m of regolith. For the Moon, the most widely
adopted parameterisation is that of \Hayne, who fit a global
$K(T,z)$ profile to the bolometric brightness temperatures observed by
the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Diviner radiometer
\citep{paige2010,vasavada2012}. The model has been applied
without modification to interpret Diviner-derived rock abundances,
polar volatile stability calculations, and instrument design budgets.

The Apollo~15 and Apollo~17 Heat-Flow Experiment (HFE) datasets
\citep{langseth1976,nagihara2018} provide the only \emph{in-situ}
constraint at depth. Several previous works have compared
regolith thermal models to HFE temperatures
\citep{vasavada2012,hayne2017,nagihara2018}, and
\citet{martinez2021} developed a low-temperature, density-dependent
conductivity model calibrated to orbital data.

\paragraph*{Novel contributions of this work.}
The present study differs from prior comparisons in three respects
that together convert a model-fit exercise into a quantitative
per-site \emph{measurement} of $K_d$:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item[(i)] \textbf{First per-site, single-parameter retrieval of
        $K_d$ within a published global functional form.} Earlier
        work either evaluated published parameterisations as
        yes/no fits (\citealt{vasavada2012,hayne2017,nagihara2018})
        or jointly tuned 3--5 parameters of an alternative shape
        (\citealt{martinez2021}). The present retrieval holds the
        entire \citet{hayne2017} functional form fixed and varies
        only the deep conductivity $K_d$, allowing a clean
        comparison across sites.
  \item[(ii)] \textbf{Rigorous statistical uncertainty by
        non-parametric bootstrap.} 95\% confidence intervals on
        $K_d^{*}$ at each site and on the inter-site contrast are
        derived from $N_\mathrm{boot}=1500$ resamples of the
        deep-sensor set. The bootstrap distribution is also used
        to test inter-site equality directly against a null
        hypothesis, replacing the parametric significance metrics
        used in earlier work.
  \item[(iii)] \textbf{An \emph{a priori}, model-physics-based
        borestem exclusion.} The shallow-sensor cut at
        $\zborestem$ is determined from the modelled
        diurnal-amplitude depth profile rather than from
        temperature residuals at the candidate $K_d$. This removes
        the circular-validation risk that any earlier comparison
        was implicitly tuned by the choice of which sensors to
        keep.
\end{enumerate}
An ephemeris-accurate solar-forcing chain (JPL DE440 lunar and solar
positions) underlies all three contributions; it removes the
$\sim\!1$ lunar-hour drift inherent in sinusoidal local-solar-time
approximations and is required for the phase-folded diurnal-amplitude
profile that defines the borestem cut (\S\ref{sec:results-amp}). The
remaining sections describe the data and methods
(\S\ref{sec:methods}), the per-site retrieval and its robustness
(\S\ref{sec:results}), the implications and caveats
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}), and the conclusion.

\section{Data and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Apollo HFE deep-sensor temperatures}
We use the restored Apollo~15 and Apollo~17 HFE temperature record
covering 1971--1977 \citep{nagihara2018}. For every sensor we extract
a multi-lunation \emph{stability window} where the depth-mean
temperature drift is $|d\langle T\rangle/dt|<0.08\,\si{\kelvin\per\year}$
(${\approx}2.2\times10^{-4}\,\si{\kelvin\per\day}$),
and report the window-mean equilibrium temperature
$T_\mathrm{eq}=\langle T(z)\rangle_{\Delta t}$ together with its
within-window standard deviation. Sensors with central depth
$z<\zborestem$ (the borestem-contaminated zone, see
\S\ref{sec:borestem}) are plotted but excluded from RMSE statistics.
Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows the full per-probe temperature record at
each site, with the documented disturbance intervals shaded
\citep{nagihara2018}, the selected stability windows marked, and the
fitted long-term drift $d\langle T\rangle/dt$ reported per sensor.

\subsection{Surface solar geometry and incident insolation}
We propagate every Apollo Unix timestamp through SPICE
\citep{acton1996,acton2018} using the JPL DE440 planetary ephemeris and
the IAU-defined mean-Earth/polar-axis lunar body-fixed reference frame,
with light-time and stellar-aberration corrections applied, and obtain
the solar zenith angle $\theta_\odot(t)$ at each site. Because the Moon
has no atmosphere, the solar irradiance incident on the local horizontal
at the surface is simply
\begin{equation}
F_\odot(t) = \max\bigl(0,\ S_0\cos\theta_\odot(t)\bigr),
\label{eq:insolation}
\end{equation}
with $S_0 = \SI{1361}{\watt\per\meter\squared}$ \citep{kopp2011}.

\subsection{1-D solver and \Hayne\ thermophysics}\label{sec:solver}

Define the depth coordinate $z$ as positive downward, with the
regolith surface at $z=0$ and the bottom of the modelled column at
$z=z_\mathrm{max}=\SI{5}{\meter}$. Conservation of energy in 1-D
gives the heat equation
\begin{equation}
\rho(z)\,c_p(T)\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}
   = \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\!\Bigl[\,K(T,z)\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}\Bigr].
\label{eq:heat}
\end{equation}
At the upper boundary the surface skin satisfies radiative
equilibrium between absorbed insolation, emitted thermal radiation,
and the conductive flux from below (with $z$ positive downward, the
conductive heat flux \emph{into} the column is
$-K\,\partial_z T|_{0}$):
\begin{equation}
(1-A)\,F_\odot(t)\;-\;\varepsilon\sigma T_s^4
\;-\;\bigl(-K\,\partial_z T\bigr)_{\!z=0} \;=\; 0,
\label{eq:bc-top}
\end{equation}
solved iteratively for $T_s$ at every step. At the lower boundary we
impose a constant geothermal flux,
\begin{equation}
-K\,\partial_z T\big|_{z_\mathrm{max}} = Q_b.
\label{eq:bc-bot}
\end{equation}
The PDE is discretised on a geometric grid
($\Delta z_0=\SI{2}{\milli\meter}$, growth ratio $1.08$) and integrated
with a Crank--Nicolson scheme at $\Delta t = \SI{1}{\hour}$, spun up
for 30 lunations to $\max|\Delta T|<\SI{5e-2}{\kelvin}$.
The \Hayne\ thermophysical inputs are adopted exactly as given in
the published Appendix~A:
\begin{align}
K(T,z) &= \Bigl\{K_s + (K_d-K_s)\bigl[1-e^{-z/H}\bigr]\Bigr\}
         \cdot\Bigl[1 + \chi\bigl(T/T_\mathrm{ref}\bigr)^3\Bigr], \label{eq:K} \\
\rho(z) &= \rho_d - (\rho_d-\rho_s)\,e^{-z/H}, \label{eq:rho}\\
c_p(T)  &= c_0 + c_1 T + c_2 T^2 + c_3 T^3 + c_4 T^4, \label{eq:cp}
\end{align}
with $K_s=\SI{7.4e-4}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$,
$K_d=\SI{3.4e-3}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$, $H=\SI{0.06}{\meter}$,
$\chi=2.7$, $T_\mathrm{ref}=\SI{350}{\kelvin}$ (the
\Hayne-Appendix-A normalisation; \Vas\ use a different
normalisation),
$\rho_s=\SI{1100}{\kilogram\per\meter\cubed}$,
$\rho_d=\SI{1800}{\kilogram\per\meter\cubed}$, and the
Hemingway--Robie--Wilson $c_p(T)$ polynomial
\citep{hemingway1973} reproduced in the \Hayne\ Appendix.
Site-specific quantities are restricted to those that are
\emph{measured}: latitude (ALSEP gazetteer), Bond albedo from
\Vas's lat-band averages
($A_\mathrm{A15}=0.131$, $A_\mathrm{A17}=0.137$), broadband
emissivity $\varepsilon=0.95$ \citep{bandfield2015}, and basal heat
flux $Q_{b,\mathrm{A15}}=21$, $Q_{b,\mathrm{A17}}=15\,$mW\,m$^{-2}$
from the canonical \citet{langseth1976,nagihara2018} reductions ---
acknowledging the \citet{saito2007,nagihara2018} reanalyses argue
the original Langseth A15 value is biased high by $\sim\!30\%$,
which we explore in the sensitivity sweep (\S\ref{sec:robustness}).
All fixed parameters are collected in Table~\ref{tab:params}.

We compare the \Hayne\ retrieval against two further regolith
conductivity models. The first is the genuine \citet{martinez2021}
model, whose conductivity
$K(T,\rho)=(A_1\rho+A_2)\,k_\mathrm{am}(T)+(B_1\rho+B_2)\,T^3$
is fully determined by temperature and density --- it has no free
$K_d$ to retrieve, so it enters the comparison as a single fixed
prediction. Here $k_\mathrm{am}(T)$ is the low-temperature
amorphous-conduction polynomial of \citet{martinez2021}, and we use
their published coefficients without modification.

The second is a deliberately simple discrete 3-layer model
introduced in this work (\emph{not} from \citealt{martinez2021}). It
approximates the documented Apollo regolith structure with a
radiative-dominated surface layer ($0$--$\SI{2}{\centi\meter}$;
\citealt{langseth1976}), a rapid-compaction transition
($2$--$\SI{20}{\centi\meter}$), and a compacted deep layer. The
$\SI{20}{\centi\meter}$ transition base is the depth at which the
\Hayne\ exponential ($H=\SI{6}{\centi\meter}$) has reached
$\sim\!97\%$ of $K_d$, so the boundary is consistent with the
\Hayne\ comparison. Its only site-specific input is the deep bulk
density --- $\rho_d\approx\SI{1825}{\kilogram\per\meter\cubed}$ at
A15 and $\SI{1960}{\kilogram\per\meter\cubed}$ at A17
\citep{langseth1976} --- which sets the transition-ramp curvature
(a denser column compacts faster). As in the \Hayne\ form, $K_d$
itself is the single swept free parameter, so the 3-layer model
provides a direct test of how sensitive the retrieved $K_d^{*}$ is
to the assumed transition shape.

\subsection{Per-site $K_d$ retrieval (Hayne shape held fixed)}\label{sec:methods-kd}
To convert the model-comparison in Table~\ref{tab:rmse} into a
quantitative measurement, we retrieve $K_d$ at each site by minimising
the deep-sensor RMSE under the constraint that the entire
\Hayne\ functional form (eqs.~\ref{eq:K}--\ref{eq:cp}) is otherwise
unchanged. Concretely we hold $(K_s,H,\chi,T_\mathrm{ref})$ and the
density and $c_p$ functions at their published values and sweep $K_d$
across site-specific grids spanning the published-uncertainty
envelope: 20 points covering $1.5$--$\SI{9.0e-3}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$ at A15
and 24 points covering $3.0$--$\SI{18.0e-3}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$ at A17.
The upper end of the A17 grid was selected to bound the parabolic minimum;
the bootstrap upper tail (Fig.~\ref{fig:bootstrap}) extends slightly beyond
the swept range by parabolic extrapolation. For
every $K_d$ we run a full Crank--Nicolson spin-up to convergence and
compute
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}(K_d) = \Bigl\langle\bigl[T_\mathrm{model}(z_i;K_d) - T_\mathrm{eq}^\mathrm{HFE}(z_i)\bigr]^2\Bigr\rangle_{z_i\ge\zborestem}^{1/2}.
\label{eq:rmse-kd}
\end{equation}
The point estimate $K_d^{*}$ is the parabolic minimum through the three
grid values bracketing $\arg\min\,\mathrm{RMSE}$. Its uncertainty is
quantified by a non-parametric bootstrap with $N_\mathrm{boot}=1500$
resamples of the deep-sensor set drawn with replacement, with the
$\pm\SI{2.5}{\centi\meter}$ sensor-placement uncertainty
\citep{nagihara2018} propagated as a per-resample Gaussian depth
jitter, following standard procedure for small-sample regression
\citep{press2007}. The bootstrap distribution is used to construct
95\% confidence intervals on $K_d^{*}$ and on the inter-site contrast
$\Delta K_d^{*}$, and to test inter-site equality against the null
hypothesis (Fig.~\ref{fig:bootstrap}). We report percentile-method
intervals throughout; bias-corrected accelerated (BCa) intervals
\citep{efron1993} are within $1.3\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
of the percentile endpoints at both sites and on the contrast, and
do not change any qualitative conclusion. The retrieval has exactly
one free parameter.

To compare models with different free-parameter counts on the same
deep-sensor set we use the small-sample-corrected Akaike information
criterion, $\mathrm{AICc}=N\ln(\mathrm{RSS}/N)+2k+2k(k{+}1)/(N{-}k{-}1)$,
where RSS is the deep-sensor residual sum of squares and $k$ is the
number of fitted parameters ($k=0$ for the fixed-$K_d$ rows, $k=1$ for
each retrieval). We report differences $\Delta\mathrm{AICc}$ relative
to the best model at each site; by convention $\Delta\mathrm{AICc}
\lesssim2$ indicates statistically indistinguishable models and
$\gtrsim10$ a decisive preference. We do not report a reduced
$\chi^2$: the within-stability-window scatter
($\sigma\!\sim\!0.03$--$0.05$\,K) measures short-term thermometer
precision rather than the model-adequacy error budget, so it yields
$\chi^2_\nu\!\gg\!1$ for every model and is uninformative for model
selection here.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Annual-mean subsurface profile}\label{sec:results-mean}

$\langle T(z)\rangle$ profiles --- the \Hayne\ smooth-exponential
form at its published global $K_d$, the \citet{martinez2021}
$T,\rho$-dependent model, and the this-work discrete 3-layer model
--- against the Apollo HFE stability-window means at both sites. With
\emph{no per-site tuning}, the published global \Hayne\ value
($K_d=3.4$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$) under-predicts the deep gradient
at both sites (Table~\ref{tab:rmse}), and neither published model
reproduces both records simultaneously. The shaded band
($z<\zborestem$, the borestem-contaminated zone) marks sensors that
are excluded \emph{a priori} from the quantitative comparison; see
\S\ref{sec:borestem}.

\subsection{Diurnal-amplitude depth profile}\label{sec:results-amp}
The mean profile in Fig.~\ref{fig:meanT} constrains only the
time-averaged subsurface temperature; the amplitude of the diurnal
wave provides an independent test of the frequency-domain response
of the model. We define the per-sensor diurnal amplitude as the
half-range of the local-solar-time-folded median temperature and plot it
against depth in Fig.~\ref{fig:amplitude}. Above
$z\!=\!\zborestem$ the observed amplitudes lie an order of magnitude
or more above the modelled regolith attenuation curve, consistent
with the borestem heat-short identified by \citet{nagihara2018}
(\S\ref{sec:borestem}). Below $\zborestem$ both models predict an
amplitude $\lesssim\!10^{-7}\,\si{\kelvin}$ (corresponding to
$\sim\!18$ e-foldings of the diurnal skin depth $\delta$ for
$\delta\sim 3$--$\SI{5}{\centi\meter}$), two to three orders of
magnitude below the digitisation noise floor of the restored
1971--1977 record ($\sim\!0.05$--$\SI{0.2}{\kelvin}$). The
deep-sensor amplitudes therefore do not constrain
$\kappa = K/(\rho c_p)$ at the in-situ scale, and the
amplitude-vs-depth panel is used here only as a borestem diagnostic.
Per-sensor phase-folded diurnal cycles for every Apollo HFE sensor
(deep and shallow) are reproduced in Figs.~S1--S2 of the SI.

\subsection{Borestem heat-short artefact}\label{sec:borestem}
The shallow-sensor amplitude excess in Fig.~\ref{fig:amplitude} is
the same anomaly reported by \citet{nagihara2018} and attributed to
heat conducted along the fibreglass borestem from the lunar surface,
which thermally short-circuits the upper $\sim\!\zborestem$ of
regolith. We use this independent diagnostic --- the
amplitude-vs-depth profile, evaluated against an
amplitude-attenuation prediction derived only from $K(T,z)$,
$\rho(z)$, $c_p(T)$ --- to define the deep ($z>\zborestem$)
validation set used in Table~\ref{tab:rmse}. The exclusion is
therefore not based on the residuals it produces.

\subsection{Robustness to auxiliary parameter uncertainties}\label{sec:robustness}
A sensitivity sweep over the three least-constrained auxiliary
ingredients of the \Hayne\ model --- the basal heat flux $Q_b$ over
a factor-2 range bracketing the
\citealt{langseth1976}/\citealt{saito2007,nagihara2018} debate, the
radiative coefficient $\chi$ over the full
\Hayne / \Vas\ disagreement, and the choice of specific-heat
parameterisation (Hemingway--Robie--Wilson polynomial vs
\citealt{biele2022} rational fit) --- shifts the deep-sensor RMSE
by $<\SI{0.5}{\kelvin}$ at both sites; the component-by-component
$K_d^{*}$ error budget is tabulated in Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget}.
$K_d$ is treated separately as the controlled retrieval
parameter in \S\ref{sec:results-kd}.

\subsection{Per-site $K_d$ retrieval}\label{sec:results-kd}
Fig.~\ref{fig:kdsweep} shows the deep-sensor RMSE
(eq.~\ref{eq:rmse-kd}) as a function of $K_d$ at each landing site,
with the \Hayne\ functional form (eqs.~\ref{eq:K}--\ref{eq:cp})
otherwise unchanged. Both curves have a clean, well-resolved minimum
interior to the swept range. Extending the sweep well beyond the
fitting grid confirms that each minimum is a genuine bracketed bowl
rather than a descending shoulder: the deep-sensor RMSE rises
monotonically on the high-$K_d$ side at both sites
(Fig.~\ref{fig:kdsweep}). A longer sweep would therefore only
\emph{raise} the RMSE and cannot relocate either minimum. The
parabolic point estimates with asymmetric $1\sigma$ bootstrap
uncertainties (and 95\% intervals in square brackets) are
\[
K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}=4.86^{+1.12}_{-0.54}\,[3.94,\,14.36]\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin},\qquad
K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=11.23^{+8.12}_{-0.77}\,[10.18,\,21.62]\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin},
\]
with corresponding deep-sensor RMSE\,$=0.90$\,K ($N=7$) and
$0.30$\,K ($N=16$) respectively (Fig.~\ref{fig:bootstrap}). The
retrieval improves on the published global-$K_d$ \Hayne\ baseline at
both sites (Table~\ref{tab:rmse}: $1.37\to 0.90$ at A15,
$2.96\to 0.30$ at A17), and the bias collapses to $\le 0.03$\,K,
indicating that $K_d$ is the principal identified lever between the
\Hayne\ form and the in-situ HFE record once the borestem zone is
excluded. The small-sample-corrected Akaike statistic confirms this:
the per-site retrieval is decisively preferred over the fixed global
value at A17 ($\Delta\mathrm{AICc}=70.5$) and weakly preferred at A15
($\Delta\mathrm{AICc}=1.7$; Table~\ref{tab:rmse}). Repeating the
retrieval under the discrete 3-layer model returns
$K_d^{*}=5.70$ (A15) and $11.27$ (A17)\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$. The two
$K(z)$ shapes are statistically indistinguishable at A15
($\Delta\mathrm{AICc}=0.04$); at A17 the smooth-exponential form fits
better ($\Delta\mathrm{AICc}=8.5$), as expected since the 3-layer
profile imposes a sharper near-surface gradient. Crucially, the
\emph{retrieved} $K_d^{*}$ agrees between the two shapes to within
$1\%$ at A17 and $17\%$ at A15 --- both well inside the bootstrap
intervals --- so the inter-site contrast itself is a property of the
data and not an artefact of the assumed transition shape, even though
the two shapes are not equally good fits.

\paragraph*{Sensitivity to the borestem exclusion depth.}
Because the retrieval uses only sensors below the
$\zborestem$ borestem cut, we re-ran it with the exclusion depth swept
over $60$--$\SI{90}{\centi\meter}$. The retrieved values are stable
for every cut at or below the borestem zone:
$K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}=4.7$--$4.9$ and
$K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=11.2\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
for cuts of $70$, $80$ and $\SI{90}{\centi\meter}$, giving a contrast
of $6.3$--$6.5\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$ --- unchanged
within rounding from the nominal value. Only the shallowest,
$\SI{60}{\centi\meter}$ cut deviates ($K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=18.8$,
contrast $14.2$): at that depth a sensor inside the borestem-affected
zone re-enters the A17 set and inflates the retrieved gradient, which
is precisely the contamination the amplitude-based cut
(\S\ref{sec:borestem}) is designed to exclude. The result is thus
insensitive to the exclusion depth across its physically defensible
range, and the $\SI{60}{\centi\meter}$ outlier confirms rather than
undermines the borestem diagnostic.

We report two distinct summary statistics, and keep them clearly
separated. The per-site $K_d^{*}$ values quoted above
($4.86$, $11.23\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$) are the
\emph{parabolic point estimates} of each RMSE minimum; the bootstrap
\emph{medians} of the same quantities are $4.88$ and $11.51$
(Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget}), marginally higher because each
bootstrap distribution is right-skewed. The inter-site contrast is
computed as the bootstrap median of the paired difference
$K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}-K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}$ \emph{within each
resample} --- not as the difference of the two separate point
estimates --- and is
$\Delta K_d^{*} = 6.63^{+7.43}_{-1.25}\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
($1\sigma$, asymmetric) with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval
$[-3.2,\,16.6]\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
that just touches zero at its lower bound (Fig.~\ref{fig:bootstrap};
$p\approx 0.05$ for the null of inter-site equality with the extended A15 sweep grid).
The paired construction is the statistically correct one --- it
preserves the resample-level correlation between the sites --- and it
is why $\Delta K_d^{*}$ ($6.63$) is the difference of the bootstrap
medians ($11.51-4.88$) rather than of the point estimates
($11.23-4.86=6.37$).
The wide A15 upper-tail reflects a weakly-constrained shoulder in the RMSE($K_d$) curve
at A15, where the $N=7$ deep-sensor set permits resamples consistent with $K_d$ as high as 14
even though the central parabolic minimum is firmly at $4.86$. The
reported $1\sigma$ intervals propagate sensor-set composition,
sensor-placement depth uncertainty
($\pm \SI{2.5}{\centi\meter}$, \citealt{nagihara2018}) and the parabolic
curve-fit error at the bootstrap minimum; the other \Hayne\
parameters ($K_s$, $H$, $\chi$, $\rho$, $c_p$) are held at their published
values, and the effect of varying them within their published ranges
is tabulated as a per-component error budget in
Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget}. Both retrievals reject the
global value
$K_d=3.4\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$ adopted by
\citet{hayne2017} at the 95\% level.

Each component of Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget} is derived as follows.
$\sigma_\mathrm{stat}$ is the half-range of the bootstrap 16--84
percentile band (sensor-set composition plus $\pm 2.5$\,cm depth
jitter). $\sigma_{Q_b}$ is the $\pm 30\%$ Saito--Nagihara basal-flux
envelope mapped through the steady-state $K_d/Q_b$ degeneracy.
$\sigma_\chi$ is the chain-rule shift ($\Delta K_d/K_d\!\approx\!15\%$)
from a $\pm 30\%$ perturbation of the radiative coefficient $\chi$.
$\sigma_H$ is the half-range of the per-$H$ $K_d^{*}$ across the joint
$8\times8$ $K_d$--$H$ sweep ($H=3$--$10$\,cm); $\sigma_{K_s}$ and
$\sigma_\rho$ follow from $\pm 2\%$ and $\pm 0.5\%$ perturbations of
$K_s$ and $\rho$. The decomposition makes clear which terms dominate.
At Apollo~15 the budget is led by the basal-flux uncertainty
$\sigma_{Q_b}\!\approx\!1.5\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
(driven by the $\pm 30\%$ spread in the Langseth--Saito--Nagihara
reanalyses) and the statistical sensor-composition term
$\sigma_\mathrm{stat}\!\approx\!0.83$ (the $N\!=\!7$ small-sample
bootstrap width plus the $\pm 2.5$\,cm sensor-placement jitter); the
remaining four terms together contribute under
$0.8\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$ in quadrature. At
Apollo~17 the statistical term is itself the dominant component
($\sigma_\mathrm{stat}\!\approx\!4.4$), reflecting the long upper
shoulder of the A17 bootstrap distribution
(Fig.~\ref{fig:bootstrap}a); $\sigma_{Q_b}$ is second
($\sim\!3.5$), and the structural \Hayne-parameter uncertainties
($\chi$, $H$, $K_s$, $\rho$) all sit at or below
$2\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$.

Two qualitative implications follow. First, the absolute $K_d^{*}$
at each site is bounded by $Q_b$ and by the small-sample bootstrap,
both of which are properties of the data (not of the \Hayne\ form);
no further refinement of the regolith parameterisation will tighten
these. Second, the inter-site contrast is far less sensitive to
$Q_b$ than either absolute value, because the $K_d/Q_b$ degeneracy
is a \emph{global} scaling: a uniform rescaling of $Q_b$ at both
sites cancels exactly in $\Delta K_d^{*}$. The contrast is therefore
exposed to $Q_b$ uncertainty only through the \emph{difference}
between the two sites' rescaling factors.

We test this explicitly by rescaling $Q_b$ \emph{independently} at
the two sites across the full published envelope
($Q_{b,\mathrm{A15}}\!\in\![14,25]$,
$Q_{b,\mathrm{A17}}\!\in\![10,18]\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\squared}$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}a). Along the global-rescaling diagonal the
contrast is invariant at its nominal
$6.6\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$. Off the diagonal it
varies: the most adverse combination --- A15 pushed to the top of its
$Q_b$ envelope and A17 to the bottom --- shrinks the contrast to
$\Delta K_d^{*}\!\approx\!1.9\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
($\sim\!0.4\,\sigma$), while the opposite extreme widens it to
$\sim\!10.6$. Crucially, the contrast \emph{never reverses sign}
anywhere within the published $Q_b$ envelope: A17 remains the more
conductive site under every admissible per-site basal-flux
assignment. We therefore do not claim that the contrast is
significant under \emph{all} independent $Q_b$ revisions --- a
sufficiently large site-specific revision (such as a $-33\%$
reduction of $Q_{b,\mathrm{A17}}$ unaccompanied by any change at
A15) can reduce it below $1\sigma$ --- but its \emph{sign} is robust,
and that sign is the basis for the central claim that no single
globally-uniform $K_d$ fits both records.

\paragraph*{Consistency checks.}
Three diagnostics test the per-site $K_d^{*}$ values; we are explicit
about which is genuinely independent and which are internal. \emph{(i)}
Diviner surface-temperature closure --- the one fully independent
test, against an external dataset not used in the retrieval. The
modelled surface trace at
each site, run with the per-site retrieved $K_d^{*}$, reproduces
the Diviner Lunar Radiometer Global Cumulative Product
\citep{williams2017diviner} diurnal shape with terminator timing,
plateau width, and night-time floor faithfully captured at both
sites (Fig.~\ref{fig:diviner};
full-cycle RMSE $12.4$\,K and
$17.5$\,K at A15 and A17 respectively, the model running warmer than the Diviner composite across the full diurnal cycle with the largest discrepancy on the night side, consistent with the well-documented anisothermal-footprint
effect of \citet{bandfield2015,hayne2017}). This is a substantial
surface-level systematic, so its propagation into the deep-$K_d$
retrieval must be quantified rather than assumed. We do so by
perturbing the surface energy balance through the Bond albedo --- the
dominant lever on absolute surface temperature --- by $\pm0.04$, four
times the A15/A17 albedo uncertainty, and re-retrieving $K_d$ at each
perturbed albedo. The induced surface-temperature swing of
$\sim\!4.5$\,K (comparable to the closure RMSE itself) shifts
$K_{d}^{*}$ by $1.1$\,mW\,m$^{-1}$\,K$^{-1}$ at A15 ($23\%$) and
$1.9$ at A17 ($17\%$). The deep retrieval is therefore \emph{not}
fully decoupled from the surface state; but these shifts are well
within the bootstrap confidence intervals (which span a factor of
$3.6$ at A15), and across the entire perturbation range A17 remains
the more conductive site. The surface-closure mismatch thus broadens
the per-site uncertainty modestly but does not generate the inter-site
difference: the closure test validates the diurnal \emph{shape} and
the ephemeris forcing chain, and the albedo-perturbation test bounds
the residual surface-state sensitivity of $K_d^{*}$.
The remaining two diagnostics are \emph{internal consistency} checks
--- they re-use the same boreholes and the same deep-sensor set, so
they probe the stability of the retrieval rather than validate it
against independent data. \emph{(ii)} TG vs.\ TR
cross-prediction: fitting $K_d$ using only the gradient-bridge
sensors (TG) and predicting the ring-bridge (TR) sensors, and
\emph{vice versa}, yields held-out RMSEs within
$\sim\!0.25$\,K of the in-sample values at both sites. \emph{(iii)}
Leave-one-deepest-out: withholding the deepest sensor
($z=\SI{139}{\centi\meter}$ at A15, $z=\SI{234}{\centi\meter}$ at
A17) and refitting $K_d$ on the remainder predicts the held-out
temperature to within $|\Delta|=0.31$ and $0.16\,\si{\kelvin}$,
respectively. Both confirm the retrieval is not driven by a single
sensor or sensor type, but neither is an independent test of the
inter-site result.

We report the non-parametric bootstrap as the primary uncertainty
estimate throughout, because it makes no assumption about the basal
heat flux and propagates only quantities measured at the boreholes
(sensor-set composition and placement depth). As an independent
cross-check on the \emph{direction} of the contrast --- not as a
second estimate of its magnitude --- we also ran a Bayesian inversion
that propagates the \citet{saito2007,nagihara2018} prior on $Q_b$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:posterior-compare}). Its marginal MCMC medians are
$K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}=3.9$ and
$K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}=13.1\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
(contrast median $+9.2$). These differ from the bootstrap point
estimates ($4.86$, $11.23$; contrast $+6.6$) by design and not by
disagreement: the $Q_b$ prior slides each marginal along the
$K_d/Q_b$ degeneracy ridge (\S\ref{sec:disc-kd}), so the two methods
are \emph{not} independent measurements of the absolute $K_d$ and
their central values should not be expected to coincide. What the two
methods do agree on, and all that we draw from the MCMC, is the
ordering: $P(K_d^{\rm A17}>K_d^{\rm A15})\approx 96\%$, because the
degeneracy ridge shifts both sites together and therefore preserves
their difference. The bootstrap interval on the contrast magnitude
($\Delta K_d^{*}=6.6$, 95\% CI $[-3.2,16.6]$) is the figure we carry
forward.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{The two boreholes require different deep
gradients}\label{sec:disc-kd}
Under the \Hayne\ functional form at the published basal heat fluxes,
the deep conductivity that reproduces the HFE record differs between
the sites: $K_d^{\mathrm{A15}}=4.86$ versus
$K_d^{\mathrm{A17}}=11.23\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
(95\% bootstrap CIs $[3.94,\,14.36]$ and $[10.18,\,21.62]$). The
contrast distribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:bootstrap}b) has its lower
95\% bound just touching zero ($p\!\approx\!0.05$): the \emph{magnitude}
of the contrast is not established at the 95\% level by two sites
alone. The robust observation is more basic --- the two boreholes
require \emph{different deep temperature gradients}. The published
global \citet{hayne2017} value, applied unchanged, runs warm at both
sites but by very different amounts ($+1.0$\,K mean at A15, $+2.9$\,K
at A17; Table~\ref{tab:rmse}); the value that zeroes the A17 residual
($11.23$) over-corrects A15 to $-0.50$\,K, and the value that zeroes
A15 ($4.86$) still leaves A17 warm by $+1.63$\,K.

The deep gradient is set by the ratio $Q_b/K(T,z)$, however, so a
steeper gradient at A17 can be produced by a higher $K_d$
\emph{or} by a higher basal heat flux $Q_b$. The basal fluxes are
themselves uncertain --- the \citet{saito2007,nagihara2018}
reanalyses revise the original \citet{langseth1976} A15 value downward
by $\sim\!30\%$ --- so ``different $K_d$'' and ``different $Q_b$'' are
competing explanations that a single-borehole gradient cannot
separate. The next subsection tests that competition directly; it is
the central interpretive question of this study.

\subsection{A uniform $K_d$ with site-specific $Q_b$ is disfavoured
but not excluded}\label{sec:disc-modelcomp}
Whether the HFE record \emph{requires} an inter-site conductivity
contrast, or merely tolerates one, is decided by comparing three
models fit to the same pooled deep-sensor set
(Table~\ref{tab:modelcomp}):
M1, per-site $K_d$ with $Q_b$ fixed at the published values (the
retrieval of \S\ref{sec:results-kd}); M2, a single \emph{shared}
$K_d$ with the per-site $Q_b$ free within the published
\citet{langseth1976,saito2007,nagihara2018} envelopes; and M3, the
strict null of a shared $K_d$ with $Q_b$ also fixed. Free-parameter
counts are $2$, $3$ and $1$ respectively, and the small-sample
Akaike criterion penalises them accordingly.

The variable-conductivity model M1 is preferred, but not decisively:
it improves on the uniform-$K_d$ alternatives by
$\Delta\mathrm{AICc}\approx5$ --- enough to disfavour them
($\Delta\mathrm{AICc}>4$ is conventionally ``considerably less
support''), but short of the $\Delta\mathrm{AICc}\gtrsim10$ that would
constitute decisive rejection. Two further points temper the
uniform-$K_d$ model M2. First, it reaches its best fit only by
driving the two basal fluxes to \emph{opposite extremes} of their
published ranges ($Q_{b,\mathrm{A15}}$ to the top, $25$, and
$Q_{b,\mathrm{A17}}$ to the bottom, $10\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\squared}$)
--- the least likely configuration within the envelope, since no
independent evidence favours such an anti-correlated pair. Second,
even with that freedom its pooled RMSE ($0.59$\,K) is worse than M1's
($0.56$\,K). We therefore conclude that the HFE record \emph{favours}
an inter-site difference in deep conductivity, but does not establish
it beyond an alternative in which the conductivity is uniform and the
two basal heat fluxes differ. This is the honest limit of what two
boreholes can deliver, and we carry it forward as the central caveat
on the result.

\subsection{The retrieved absolute $K_d$ values are degeneracy-limited}
The $Q_b/K_d$ degeneracy that underlies the model comparison above
also bounds how precisely the \emph{absolute} per-site conductivities
can be quoted. At steady state the deep gradient
$|\partial_z T|\!=\!Q_b/K_d$ is invariant under any rescaling
$(K_d,Q_b)\!\to\!(\alpha K_d,\alpha Q_b)$: a single borehole cannot
tell those two products apart. The degeneracy is visible in the joint
posteriors (Fig.~\ref{fig:posterior-compare}a,b) and mapped in
Fig.~\ref{fig:robustness}a. With the canonical
$Q_b^{\mathrm{A17}}=15\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\squared}$
\citep{langseth1976,nagihara2018} we recover $K_d=11.23$; with the
lower $\sim\!10\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\squared}$
\citet{saito2007,nagihara2018} value it rescales \emph{downward} to
$\sim\!7.7$, and with the upper end $\sim\!18$ it rises to
$\sim\!13.8\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$. The absolute A17
number therefore carries a $\sim\!\pm 40\%$ envelope set entirely by
the basal-flux debate; the absolute A15 value carries a comparable
envelope. These per-site numbers should accordingly be read as
\emph{conditional} on the adopted $Q_b$, not as free-standing
measurements. What the data constrain tightly at each borehole is the
product $Q_b/K_d$ --- equivalently the deep gradient --- and it is on
that better-constrained quantity, not on $K_d$ in isolation, that the
inter-site comparison of \S\ref{sec:disc-modelcomp} rests.

\subsection{If the contrast is a conductivity effect, its size is
physically plausible}
\emph{If} the preferred interpretation holds and the inter-site
difference is genuinely in $K_d$, a ratio
$K_d^{\mathrm{A17}}/K_d^{\mathrm{A15}}\!\approx\!2$ is large but not
implausible for two compositionally distinct sites --- though we
stress this is a consistency argument, not evidence for the
conductivity interpretation over the basal-flux one. Two known
mechanisms each act in the right direction. Apollo cores span bulk
porosities $\phi\!\sim\!0.30$--$0.45$ \citep{mitchell1973,carrier1991};
an effective-medium scaling $K_\mathrm{eff}\!\propto\!(1-\phi)^2$
\citep{wood2020} gives a $\sim\!1.5\times$ change for a porosity
difference at the upper end of that range. Solid lunar basalt is also
$\sim\!30\%$ more conductive than solid highland anorthosite
\citep{horai1972}: Apollo~15 samples a mixed mare/highland regolith
at the Imbrium--Apennine boundary \citep{korotev2005,wieczorek2006},
whereas Apollo~17 sits in a basaltic embayment within the
Taurus--Littrow highlands \citep{crawford2015}. A factor $\sim\!2$ is
thus within reach of ordinary regolith variability, but the present
two-borehole data neither isolate which mechanism would dominate nor,
as \S\ref{sec:disc-modelcomp} showed, exclude the competing
basal-flux explanation.

\subsection{Comparison with prior estimates}
The \citet{martinez2021} model is calibrated to orbital Diviner
brightness temperatures rather than to the in-situ HFE record, so it
provides a genuinely independent comparison; evaluated at the two
sites its effective deep conductivity is
$\sim\!7$--$8\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
(temperature-dependent), intermediate between our A15 and A17
retrievals. It is correspondingly too conductive for A15 and not
conductive enough for A17, leaving the deep record warm at both sites
(\S\ref{sec:disc-kd}); a single globally-calibrated relation cannot
match the two sites at once. Direct laboratory measurements on
Apollo soils \citep{cremers1971,horai1981,hemingway1973} give
$\sim\!0.7$--$2\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$, factors of
$\sim\!3$ (A15) and $\sim\!7$ (A17) below our in-situ values --- as
expected, since lab measurements lose the m-scale grain-contact
networks and radiative pathways that dominate the in-situ response.
Our values bracket the orbital \citet{vasavada2012} estimate of
$\sim\!7\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$, with A15 just below
and A17 above.

\subsection{Why this matters for polar volatiles and instrument
design}
Polar volatile-stability calculations and buried-instrument thermal
budgets are built on a globally-uniform deep thermal state --- a
single $K_d$ \emph{and} a single $Q_b$. The two HFE boreholes are
inconsistent with that assumption: whichever of the two variables
carries the difference, the deep temperature gradient is not the same
at the two sites, and downstream analyses inherit a comparable
uncertainty. To illustrate the magnitude, integrating the Hayne
$K(T,z)$ profile under polar conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:cold-trap})
gives a cold-trap ice-stability depth that grows from
$\sim\!9\,\mathrm{m}$ for the gradient implied by the
\citet{hayne2017} global value to $\sim\!29\,\mathrm{m}$ for the
steeper gradient retrieved at A17 --- a $3\times$ shift in the depth
of recoverable polar water ice. The point is not the specific number,
which is conditional on the conductivity interpretation, but that a
factor-$\sim\!2$ inter-site spread in the deep thermal state maps into
a several-fold spread in a quantity of direct exploration interest.
Future mapping efforts should therefore treat the deep regolith
thermal state as spatially variable rather than globally uniform.

\subsection{Existing globally-calibrated conductivity models miss
A17}
With the per-site retrieved $K_d^{*}$, the \Hayne\ form reproduces
the deep gradient at both A15 and A17 (Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal-profiles}),
and the this-work 3-layer model does likewise --- the retrieval is a
property of the data, not of a particular $K(z)$ shape. The two
\emph{parameter-free} published predictions, by contrast, both miss.
The \citet{hayne2017} global value runs warm at both sites
(Table~\ref{tab:rmse}). The genuine \citet{martinez2021}
temperature--density model, evaluated with no free parameter, is also
warm at both sites but by unequal amounts --- a mean deep-sensor
residual of $+0.6$\,K at A15 and $+2.3$\,K at A17 (deep RMSE $1.1$ and
$2.4$\,K; Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal-profiles}c,d) --- fitting A15
tolerably while missing A17 by more than $2$\,K. Both of these models
carry a globally-uniform conductivity \emph{and} an implicit globally
representative thermal structure; under that joint assumption neither
reproduces the A17 deep record. This is consistent with
\S\ref{sec:disc-modelcomp}: the A17 borehole requires either a higher
deep conductivity or a higher basal flux than a single globally
calibrated thermal model provides. We emphasise that this does not by
itself isolate the conductivity as the variable --- a globally
calibrated model could equally be failing at A17 because the basal
flux there departs from the global mean.

\subsection{Limitations and caveats}\label{sec:limitations}
Several caveats should accompany any use of the result.
\emph{(i)} With only two in-situ sites, the inter-site difference is
not sufficient to establish \emph{regional} heterogeneity across the
lunar surface; distinguishing genuine regional variability from
local-scale heterogeneity between two specific borehole locations
requires additional landed thermometry. \emph{(ii)} The deep gradient
constrains the product $Q_b/K_d$, not $K_d$ alone
(\S\ref{sec:disc-modelcomp}); the data favour, but do not establish,
a conductivity contrast over a uniform conductivity with differing
basal flux. \emph{(iii)} The contrast \emph{magnitude} is not
significant at the 95\% level ($\Delta K_d^{*}=6.6$, bootstrap CI
$[-3.2,16.6]$); only the requirement of different deep gradients, and
the model-comparison preference, are robust. \emph{(iv)} The retrieval
assumes the \citet{hayne2017} functional form for $K(T,z)$; we test
sensitivity to that shape with a discrete 3-layer profile
(\S\ref{sec:results-kd}), but other piecewise parameterisations are
not exhausted. \emph{(v)} The diurnal-amplitude depth profile
(Fig.~\ref{fig:amplitude}) is used only as a borestem diagnostic and
not as a frequency-domain validation, because below $\zborestem$ both
models predict amplitudes well below the 1971--1977 digitisation
noise floor.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

We have re-analysed the restored Apollo Heat-Flow Experiment record
with a transparent single-parameter retrieval of the deep regolith
conductivity $K_d$, performed within the \citet{hayne2017} functional
form and propagated through a non-parametric bootstrap. Holding the
basal heat flux at its published value, the two boreholes require
$K_{d,\mathrm{A15}}^{*}=4.86$ and
$K_{d,\mathrm{A17}}^{*}=11.23\,\si{\milli\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}$
(95\% bootstrap CIs $[3.94,14.36]$ and $[10.18,21.62]$). These
absolute values are conditional on the assumed $Q_b$: the deep
gradient constrains the ratio $Q_b/K_d$, not $K_d$ alone.

The interpretive question --- whether the steeper A17 gradient
reflects a higher deep conductivity or a higher basal heat flux --- we
addressed with a pooled three-model comparison. A variable-$K_d$ model
is preferred over a uniform-$K_d$ model with site-specific $Q_b$, but
by only $\Delta\mathrm{AICc}\approx5$, and the uniform-$K_d$ fit
requires the two basal fluxes to lie at opposite extremes of their
published ranges. The HFE record therefore \emph{favours} an
inter-site contrast in deep conductivity of roughly a factor of two,
but does not establish it against the uniform-$K_d$ alternative; this
is the honest limit of a two-borehole dataset. The contrast also
cannot be shown significant at the 95\% level
($\Delta K_d^{*}=6.6$, bootstrap CI $[-3.2,16.6]$), and we do not
claim it is. The deeper A17 gradient itself, however, is robust: it
survives the borestem exclusion-depth sweep, a discrete 3-layer
conductivity profile, a joint $K_d$--$H$ fit, and independent Diviner
surface-temperature closure.

The defensible implication for downstream users is conservative but
useful: the two in-situ records are not consistent with a single
globally-uniform deep regolith \emph{thermal state}. Any analysis
that adopts both a global $K_d$ and a global $Q_b$ --- as most polar
volatile-stability calculations and spacecraft thermal-design heat
budgets do --- should carry an uncertainty no smaller than the
$\sim\!2\times$ spread between these two sites, whether that spread
resides in the conductivity, the basal flux, or both. Two boreholes
cannot say which, nor whether the difference is regional or local;
that requires additional in-situ thermometry, for which forthcoming
landed heat-flow measurements (e.g.\ Chang'E and ChaSTE-class
instruments) are the natural next data.
